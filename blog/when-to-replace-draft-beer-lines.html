When to Replace Your Draft Beer Lines

September 22, 2026 · 5 min read

Cleaning keeps draft lines sanitary, but it doesn't make them last forever. Tubing, seals, and faucets wear out, and at some point no amount of cleaning brings a line back. Knowing when to replace instead of clean saves you from chasing off-flavors and foam that won't go away.

Why Draft Lines Wear Out

Beer line tubing is slightly porous. Over time, flavor compounds soak into the walls, microscopic scratches build up on the inner surface, and residue settles into those scratches, where cleaning chemicals have a harder time reaching it. Each cleaning cycle gets a little less effective. Standard vinyl tubing is the most vulnerable. Barrier tubing, used in most long-draw trunk lines, resists this far better, but it isn't permanent either.

The General Guideline

The Brewers Association Draught Beer Quality Manual recommends replacing vinyl tubing, including jumper lines and direct-draw lines, every year. Trunk lines made of barrier tubing last considerably longer, and their replacement depends on condition rather than a fixed date. That's why regular inspection matters as much as regular cleaning.

Faucet and coupler seals (washers and o-rings) wear on their own schedule. They should be inspected every time faucets and couplers are taken apart for cleaning, and replaced as soon as they're cracked, flattened, or hardened.

Signs a Line Needs Replacing

  • Off-flavors that come back right after a cleaning. If a line tastes clean for a day or two and then goes stale, sour, or buttery again, contamination is living in the tubing walls.
  • Discoloration or visible buildup. Vinyl that has gone cloudy, yellow, or shows dark spots from the outside is holding residue inside.
  • A line that tastes like the last beer. Tubing that has absorbed flavor will pass it on. A light lager on a line that used to run an IPA can pick up hop character.
  • Stiff, cracked, or kinked tubing. Hardened vinyl cracks and holds bacteria in the cracks. Kinks cause turbulence and foam.
  • Persistent foam on one line only. When temperature and pressure check out and one line still foams, a damaged or worn line is a common cause.
  • Leaks at fittings or seals. Worn washers and o-rings leak beer and can let air into the system.

Replacing Lines Doesn't Replace Cleaning

New tubing gets contaminated on the same timeline as old tubing. Biofilm starts forming as soon as beer flows through a line. A fresh line on a skipped cleaning schedule will develop the same problems, only a bit more slowly. Replacement resets the line's condition. The 14-day cleaning cycle required in Pennsylvania is what keeps it there.

The reverse is also true: good cleaning makes lines last. Lines cleaned every two weeks with the right chemistry, plus a quarterly acid cleaning to remove beer stone, hold up far longer than lines that are cleaned irregularly and then scrubbed hard to catch up.

What Replacement Involves

Replacing jumper lines on a direct-draw system or kegerator is quick and inexpensive. Long-draw trunk lines are a bigger job because they run through walls, ceilings, or conduit, which is exactly why they're built from barrier tubing and why keeping them clean matters so much. Line length and diameter should match the original design when lines are replaced, or the system can end up unbalanced and pour foamy.
