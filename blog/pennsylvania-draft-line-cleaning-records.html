Draft Line Cleaning Records: What Pennsylvania Requires

September 22, 2026 · 5 min read

Most Pennsylvania bar owners know their draft lines have to be cleaned. Fewer know that the law also requires proof of it, and that the proof has to be on the premises. If a Liquor Control Enforcement officer walks in and asks for your cleaning records, what you hand over matters almost as much as whether the lines are actually clean.

What the Regulation Requires

Pennsylvania's draft cleaning rules are in Title 40 of the Pennsylvania Code, Chapter 5. Three sections do most of the work.

Section 5.51 sets the cleaning frequency. Dispensing lines, valves, joints, couplers, hose fittings, washers, o-rings, empty beer detectors (FOBs), and foam control units must be cleaned once every 14 days. The one exception is a licensee with an operating ultrasonic, electromagnetic, or similar system that slows yeast and bacteria growth in the lines. In that case, the manufacturer's cleaning schedule applies.

Section 5.52 covers records. When an outside company cleans your system, you have to get a certificate from the cleaner showing three things: the date of the cleaning, the name of the person who did it, and the method used. That certificate has to be kept on file at the licensed premises, available for inspection by the Board at all times.

Section 5.54 puts the responsibility on you. The licensee is solely responsible for keeping draft equipment clean and sanitary. It also says that having records showing the system was cleaned is no defense if the system is found in an unsanitary condition.

The Rule Changed in 2021: It's 14 Days, Not 7

For decades, Pennsylvania required cleaning every seven days. In July 2021, the Liquor Control Board amended Section 5.51 and moved the requirement to every 14 days. Plenty of older articles, distributor handouts, and even some legal summaries still say seven days. The current standard is 14, which also matches the Brewers Association's minimum recommendation of cleaning at least every two weeks.

Fourteen days is a ceiling, not a target. A cleaning on day 15 is a missed cleaning.

What a Good Cleaning Record Looks Like

The regulation sets the minimum: date, person, method. A record that actually protects you says more. Every certificate you get should show:

  • The date of the cleaning, so the gap between visits is obvious at a glance
  • The name of the technician who did the work, not only the company name
  • The cleaning method, such as recirculated alkaline cleaning or an acid cleaning
  • Which lines and components were cleaned, including faucets and couplers
  • Anything the technician found, such as worn seals, damaged lines, or temperature issues

If you clean in-house, record the same details yourself after every cleaning. A binder behind the bar with dated entries is enough, as long as it's current and complete.

Where Records Go Wrong

Gaps. The date column tells the story. Two entries 20 days apart show a missed cleaning, even if every other interval is perfect.

No certificate from the cleaner. If your distributor or a service company cleans your lines and leaves without giving you anything, you have nothing to show. Ask for the certificate at every visit. The regulation puts the obligation to obtain it on you, not on them.

Records kept somewhere else. An owner's email inbox or an office across town doesn't count. The certificate has to be on file at the licensed premises.

Paper that doesn't match reality. Section 5.54 is clear that records don't protect a dirty system. A log that says the lines were cleaned last week won't help if the faucets are visibly contaminated.
