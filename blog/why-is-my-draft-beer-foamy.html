Why Is My Draft Beer Foamy?

September 22, 2026 · 6 min read

Foamy draft beer is the most expensive problem behind most bars, and most operators underestimate how expensive it is. Every pint that has to be topped off, dumped, or poured into a pitcher and left to settle is beer you paid for and can't sell. A tap pouring a third foam can cost you a meaningful share of every keg. The upside is that foam has a short list of causes, and you can check them in order.

Start with Temperature

Warm beer is the single most common cause of foam. CO₂ stays dissolved in cold beer and comes out of solution fast as beer warms. The Brewers Association Draught Beer Quality Manual calls for a keg cooler held at 38°F. A few degrees above that can turn a clean pour into a glass of foam.

Check the cooler with a thermometer in a glass of water that has sat in the cooler for a while, not with the dial on the door. Then check the beer itself. If the first pour of the day is the foamiest, the beer sitting in the tower or the line has warmed overnight. On long-draw systems, that points to the glycol chiller or the insulation around the trunk line. On direct-draw kegerators, it usually means the tower isn't being cooled.

Check Your CO₂ Pressure

Pressure that's too high overcarbonates the beer in the keg over a few days, and it pours foamy no matter how cold it is. Pressure that's too low lets CO₂ break out of the beer inside the line, which creates foam pockets and sputtering pours. Both show up as foam at the faucet, so the fix depends on which direction you're off.

Pressure should be set for the beer's carbonation level and the cooler temperature, not cranked up to make a slow tap pour faster. Turning up the regulator to speed up a slow line is one of the most common ways bars create their own foam problem. If a line pours too slowly at the correct pressure, the issue is line balance, not pressure.

Line Balance and Restriction

A draft system is balanced when the resistance of the line (its length, diameter, and the height it climbs to the faucet) matches the pressure pushing the beer. When a line has too little resistance for the pressure applied, beer races out of the faucet and foams. When someone shortens a line during a repair or swaps in a different tubing size, the balance changes even though nothing looks wrong.

If one tap foams and the others on the same cooler and gas pour fine, compare that line's length and tubing to the rest. A kinked or crushed line can also cause turbulence that knocks CO₂ out of the beer.

Dirty Lines Cause Foam

Buildup inside a line gives CO₂ something to break out on. Biofilm, yeast, and beer stone create rough surfaces inside tubing that should be smooth, and each rough spot becomes a point where bubbles form. A system that used to pour well and has gradually gotten foamier over weeks is often a dirty system, especially if the foam comes with an off smell or taste.

Pennsylvania requires dispensing lines, couplers, and foam control units to be cleaned every 14 days. A skipped cycle doesn't only put you out of compliance; it's often the first thing that shows up in the glass.

Faucets, Couplers, and Seals

A faucet that's only partly opened sprays beer and makes foam. Train staff to open the faucet all the way in one motion. Worn washers and o-rings in faucets and couplers can let air into the line, and a sticky faucet that doesn't open fully causes the same problem as a bartender who doesn't. Faucets should be taken apart and hand-cleaned as part of every professional cleaning, which is also when worn seals get spotted.

Don't Overlook the Glass

Glassware that isn't beer-clean (with residue from grease, detergent, or sanitizer) causes foam and bubbles clinging to the sides. Frosted or hot glasses straight from the washer do the same. Rinse glasses in cold water before the pour. If foam only appears in some glasses, the glass is your problem, not the draft system.

A Quick Diagnostic Order

  1. Check the beer temperature in the cooler, not only the thermostat setting.
  2. Confirm regulator pressure hasn't been turned up to speed up a slow line.
  3. Compare the foaming line against lines that pour well: same length, same tubing, no kinks.
  4. Check when the lines were last cleaned.
  5. Inspect faucets and coupler seals, and watch how staff open the faucet.
  6. Pour into a rinsed, room-temperature, beer-clean glass to rule out glassware.

When to Call Someone

If temperature and pressure are right and a line still foams, you're usually looking at a balance problem, a dirty system, or worn hardware. Those are hard to chase one variable at a time. A technician can clean the system, inspect every faucet and coupler, and check whether the lines are balanced for your setup.
